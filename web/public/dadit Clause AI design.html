Etymology

From the Greek ephēmeros, "lasting only a day" — from epi- (upon) and hēmera (day). Ancient physicians used the word for fevers that came and went within a single day. By the 16th century, English writers had borrowed it for anything fleeting — flowers, beauty, life itself. The mayfly's scientific name, Ephemeroptera, still preserves the original sense: born in the morning, gone by night.

B
Warmer · illustrated
A postcard from each chapter.

Each card is a low-saturation colored surface — meanings on dusty blue, etymology on terracotta, image on vellum, take-it-further on indigo wash. The colors aren't pastels; they're the muted hues of old book ribbon, herbarium pages, faded velvet. The cards stack like postcards from different chapters of a journey. Risk: with four colored cards in a row, the page can tip from "art book" toward "pastel dashboard" if any saturation creeps up. We held it under 8%. Reward: instant scannability for returning users — color is the navigation.

C
Wildcard · editorial
One continuous monograph.

No cards. Every section sits along a single editorial thread that runs down the page like a margin rule in a printed essay. Each section is anchored to the thread by a small colored dot — that's where the color lives. Above each section sits a Roman numeral and an italic eyebrow in the section's color. The whole page reads as one typeset essay, not a stack of UI. Risk: less "scannable at a glance" than the card variants — a returning user has to read the eyebrow, not just the surface color. Reward: the most distinctive identity in the field; the one design that nothing else in the dictionary space looks like; the most adult.

Notes for the next round
My recommendation: B for the launch, C if you have nerve.

Variant B is the safest path that still has identity. The colored cards do real work — a returning user looking for "where did the etymology go" finds the rust-colored block in half a second. It's warmer than A, more conventional than C, and it scales cleanly to the rest of the app (pricing tiers, notebook entries, account screens) without major reinvention.

Variant C is the design that wins press and gets shared on Twitter. The vertical thread + Roman numerals + colored anchor dots is genuinely unlike anything in the dictionary space. The risk is real: returning users may find it harder to "scan to a section" because the color signal is smaller. But the brand differentiation is unmatched — and the same system extends beautifully to the Word Book (imagine 100 saved words, each a numbered entry on the same vertical thread).

Variant A is correct, calm, and forgettable. I built it because the brief asked for a restrained pole — but I'd push back on shipping it. With Fraunces + Inter on cream, "restrained" is one bad CSS day away from "blank." I think it loses the children entirely — there's nothing on the page that makes an 11-year-old lean forward.

Three open questions for you: (1) Should the kids card and idioms card be visible by default on mobile, or collapsed behind a "show more" tap to keep the first scroll focused? (2) The "Save to Word Book" button is teal across all variants — should it shift to the indigo of "take it further" instead, to reinforce that saving is a take-action gesture? (3) For the Word Book screen (next round), do we keep the same color-per-section system, or does the Word Book get its own monochrome treatment so it reads as "the archive" vs. "the encounter"?
